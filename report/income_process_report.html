Summary

This report estimates how American household labor income moves over a working life, following Cocco, Gomes and Maenhout (2005; "CGM"). Income has three parts: a predictable age profile; permanent shocks, which shift income for good; and transitory shocks, which last one year. We re-estimate CGM's model on the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID) from 1969 to 2022, split it by education, industry, income level and birth cohort, follow it over time, and check it against two Census surveys, SIPP and CPS ASEC. The parameters feed a life cycle model; they are in dataset/.

  • The replication holds. On CGM's own years (1969-1991) the age profiles and shock variances match theirs closely once incomes under $2,000 (1992 dollars) are dropped (Table 1).
  • Permanent risk doubled after the 1970s and has stayed up. The variance of permanent shocks went from 0.012 in 1969-1980 to 0.025 in 1981-1992 and has been about 0.02 since (Figure 1).
  • Transitory risk rose in the 1990s from about 0.065 to 0.10, and eased to about 0.08 in the 2010s (Figure 2).
  • Income now rises faster to midlife and falls less after 50. From 25 to 50 the profile rises 1.52× in 1969-1980 and 2.17× in 2007-2022 (Figure 3).
  • Less education means more transitory risk, and that gap has widened: since 2007 the transitory variance is 0.142 without a high school diploma and 0.069 with a degree. Permanent risk is now similar across groups.
  • Low earners face the largest transitory shocks and the flattest profiles; industry matters for transitory risk more than for permanent risk.
  • SIPP and CPS agree on the direction of every trend, but their panels are too short to split shocks into permanent and transitory, so the variances come from the PSID.

The model and how it is estimated

log Yit = f(t, Zit) + vit + εit,  vit = vi,t−1 + uit,  Var(u) = σ²u,  Var(ε) = σ²ε

Income is CGM's household measure: the head's and spouse's labor income plus unemployment and workers' compensation, Social Security, SSI, other welfare, child support and help from relatives, in 1992 dollars (CPI-U). Sample: the PSID's original SRC sample, male heads aged 20 to 65 (22 to 65 with a college degree), excluding the retired, housewives and students. Following the project decision of 10 October 2026, years with income under $2,000 (1992 dollars) are dropped; the full sample is the robustness check.

Age profile. A fixed-effects regression of log income on age dummies, marital status and household size, estimated separately for each group; a cubic in age is fitted to the age dummies. Because the regression has no year effects, the profile includes the average real income growth of the years it covers, as in CGM. Shocks. With rd the change in residual log income over d years, Var(rd) = d·σ²u + 2σ²ε (Carroll and Samwick 1997). The two variances are the slope and half the intercept of a regression of Var(rd) on d. Lags are calendar years, so the PSID's two-year waves after 1997 contribute even lags; long periods use lags up to 22 years, shorter ones up to 11. Standard errors come from a bootstrap over people. Replacement rate: mean income including pensions of retired heads aged 65-75, over the group's fitted income at 65.

Groups. Education: no high school diploma, high school (including some college), college degree. Industry: seven sectors from Census industry codes, the person's most common sector. Income: quintiles of a person's average income relative to others of the same age and year ("lifetime rank"), or of his first three years only ("initial rank"; variances then use later years only). Cohort: birth decade.

Replicating CGM

On CGM's sample and years we get 1,139 / 2,919 / 1,134 households against their 1,104 / 2,816 / 1,110, nearly the same marital and household-size effects, and age profiles that match theirs up to the mid-40s. Our profiles for the two non-college groups fall more after 50. Our shock variances are higher on the full sample; the gap comes from a few very low incomes (under $2,000 is 0.3% of high-school observations but 16% of the residual variance). Without them the variances are close to CGM's.

How the income process has changed

Permanent risk roughly doubled between the 1970s and the 1980s and has not come back down. Transitory risk rose later, through the 1990s, and has eased somewhat since 2008. The age profile has become steeper: income now grows more between 25 and 50 and falls less in the 50s.

By education

College graduates start lower, rise far faster and peak later (around 47); households without a high school diploma peak in their early 40s. Transitory risk falls with education, and since the 1990s the gap has widened: the no-diploma group's transitory variance has roughly doubled while the college group's is back near its 1970s level. Permanent risk was highest for college graduates in CGM's years and is now about the same for all three groups.

By industry

Industry sorts households mainly by transitory risk. Public administration and manufacturing have the smallest year-to-year swings; agriculture, mining and construction have more than twice as much. Permanent risk varies less, with finance and business services the highest. Public administration's replacement rate above 1 reflects government pensions.

By income level

Low earners face much larger transitory shocks: the transitory variance falls from 0.20 in the bottom quintile to about 0.06 at the top. Part of that is mechanical, since a bad year also lowers a person's lifetime rank; ranking on the first three years removes that link and roughly halves the gap (0.135 against 0.077). Permanent risk is about the same in every quintile under that ranking. Profiles differ more by income than by any other cut: the bottom quintile peaks in its mid-30s, the top keeps rising into its 50s, and Social Security replaces a larger share of low incomes.

By birth cohort

Each later cohort has faced larger transitory shocks, from about 0.08 for men born in the 1930s to 0.14 for those born in the 1970s, while permanent risk is 0.013-0.016 for every cohort. Because a cohort is observed only over part of its working life, this cut mixes cohort and period; the period estimates above are the cleaner view of change over time.

Checking against SIPP and CPS ASEC

SIPP and CPS measure each person's own earnings, so the comparison uses men's own earnings in all three surveys (for the PSID, the head's labor income). All three show the same changes: income rising faster to midlife, a smaller decline after 50, and larger year-to-year swings over time. Levels differ for known reasons. The CPS follows addresses, not people, and its one-year changes include matching and reporting error. SIPP's 2014 redesign raised measured volatility by about 70%, so its two designs are never pooled.

The age profiles agree on the rise and differ on the decline (Figure 10). In every survey earnings roughly double or more between 25 and 50. After 50 the PSID profile falls, while the CPS and SIPP profiles since 2007 stay near their peak to 65. The PSID follows the same men as they age; CPS and SIPP compare different men at each age, and the men still earning at 60-65 are disproportionately those with high earnings. That selection most likely flattens the survey profiles late in life, so for a model of one worker's path the PSID profile is the right one.

The split between permanent and transitory risk needs long panels. SIPP follows people for 2 to 4 years, and with only those short lags the PSID itself gives a permanent variance 1.3 to 2.2 times its long-lag estimate. The CPS method (the rise in earnings inequality with age within a cohort) gives lower values. We therefore take the shock variances from the PSID and use SIPP and CPS as checks on the profile and on volatility.

Parameters for the life cycle model

The recommended baseline is the PSID estimate by education over all years, 1969-2022, with the replacement rates chosen for the project. For a model of today's workers, the 2007-2022 set has a steeper profile and, for the less educated, larger transitory shocks. For a model without education types, use the pooled rows: one process for all households over ages 20-65, with no split by education, cohort or anything else, and a replacement rate of 0.69 measured the same way as the education rates. Every other cut and period is in the dataset.

log Y = const + married·m + hhcomp·h + c0 + c1·age + c2·age²/10 + c3·age³/100 + v + ε
retirement income = λ · exp(const + married·m̄ + hhcomp·h̄ + f(65))

Data notes and caveats

  • Sample. Male heads in the PSID's original SRC sample, unweighted, as in CGM. The low-income SEO sample and the immigrant refreshers are excluded.
  • PSID changes. Computer-assisted interviews from 1993, a new labor-income definition in 1994 (we restore business and farm labor income), transfers recorded as amounts per period in 1994-2003 (we annualize them), two-year waves from 1997, and clusters of imputed incomes in 1997 and 1999.
  • Profiles include the cycle. Like CGM's, the profiles have no year effects, so each one carries the real growth and recessions of its window. Compare shapes, not single peaks.
  • Lag range. The permanent-shock estimate depends on how many years apart the compared incomes are; the long periods use up to 22 years, the others up to 11.
  • Grouping. Industry is a person's most common sector; people who switch count in one only. Industry codes change system in 2003. Use the initial income rank for income-group variances.
  • Replacement rates. CGM's published rates (0.89 / 0.68 / 0.94) cannot be reproduced from their stated income definition; ours include pensions and are measured against the profile at 65.
